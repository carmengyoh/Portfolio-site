Designing and validating a multi-product journey around the different ways merchants understand, compare and choose funding.

Overview

As Liberis moved towards offering merchants more than one funding product, the job was no longer just helping someone complete an application. It was helping them choose without steering them towards the largest amount available.

How might we help merchants understand, compare and confidently choose the right funding product without overwhelming them or steering the decision?

I led the design end to end: framing the customer decision, synthesising research into behavioural personas, mapping the journey, prototyping competing directions and validating the proposed experience.

WHY IT WAS HARD

Product eligibility, commercial priorities and customer understanding all shaped the experience. The journey had to offer useful guidance while making it clear why an option was being suggested. It also needed enough detail for merchants to compare products without overwhelming them with financial information.

The process

I moved from understanding how merchants make funding decisions to mapping the journey, testing different levels of guidance and refining one flexible experience.

Step #1: Understanding how merchants decide

I started by combining findings from interviews with our sales team and evidence from how merchants moved through the existing funding journey. Rather than grouping people by demographics, I looked at how they approached the decision and what information they needed. Some merchants wanted clear facts and the freedom to decide independently. Others needed a more guided experience to help them make a decision. Merchants who focused on cost needed clear information about total cost and repayment flexibility.

KEY INSIGHT

The same information did not serve every merchant equally. The opportunity was one experience that could flex between independent comparison and meaningful guidance, not three disconnected journeys.

Step #2: Mapping one flexible journey

I mapped the experience before designing detailed screens so the team could see where merchants explored independently, where guidance entered the journey and how each decision led into an application. That made it easier to test the structure of the decision before debating visual details.

Step #3: Testing two initial concepts

I translated the research into two deliberately different clickable directions. Concept A tested lower guidance with fewer figures shown early in the journey. Concept B tested a guided recommendation built around a short quiz. Testing both helped separate the support merchants genuinely needed from the assumptions embedded in each approach.

What we learned from testing

All five participants preferred Prototype 2. They found the visible amounts, comparison table, examples and imagery clearer and more useful when deciding between the products. All five responded positively to seeing amounts early because it made the offer feel more concrete and easier to compare.

The quiz was useful, particularly for merchants who were less confident about their funding needs, but it did not need to be part of everyone’s journey. Three of the five participants used it naturally and four rated it four or five out of five.

WHAT STILL NEEDED WORK

Every participant wanted clearer information about fees, comparative cost or repayment before feeling ready to continue. The Flex product also needed a simpler explanation of how the limit, redrawing funds, the 12-month period and fees worked.

How I used the findings

I began bringing the strongest parts of both concepts into one more prototype to test. I kept the visible amounts, comparison table, examples and optional quiz from Prototype 2, while creating a quicker route for merchants who already knew what they wanted. I also made fees, repayment and the way Flex works clearer before asking merchants to move forward.

Step #4: Refining the experience for clarity and control

I explored multiple concepts, moving from broad journey ideas into a high-fidelity prototype. I kept the first comparison simple by showing the differences merchants needed to choose between the products. More detailed costs and repayment terms were available when they wanted to look further. When the experience suggested an option, I explained why and made it easy to return to the other choices.

Outcome

The work produced a validated direction for the future multi-product experience and gave the wider team a shared language for discussing merchant choice. Testing focused on whether people understood the differences between products, could explain why an option suited them and felt informed rather than pushed.

RESULT

A coherent multi-product direction grounded in observed decision behaviours, with comparison and recommendation treated as connected parts of one journey.

Reflection

Multi-product design is not a navigation problem. It is a decision-design problem: understanding how people weigh trade-offs, then giving them the right balance of clarity, guidance and control.

With more time, I would build earlier, lighter research checkpoints into the process and define how we would monitor confident decision-making after launch, not only application completion.
