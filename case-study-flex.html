Launching and evolving a new funding product

Flex Advance was a new reusable funding product with no Partner Hub journey. I led it from account creation to first advance through agent research, responsive design and engineering delivery. The journey launched for partner agents, then post-launch behaviour shaped a clearer funding-assessment direction and a separate preview for early cost conversations.

Overview

Flex Advance introduced a new way for merchants to access funding through a reusable limit rather than one fixed advance. Partner Hub was the platform partner sales agents used to manage merchant funding applications. Working on the merchant's behalf, agents needed to create their account, complete the funding assessment, establish the reusable Flex Advance limit and take the first advance. The experience needed to help agents understand the new product themselves while explaining its choices and costs clearly to merchants.

How might we help partner agents assess a merchant, explain how the merchant's choices affect the reusable limit and confidently set up Flex Advance on their behalf?

I owned the journey end to end: mapping the product and user dependencies, defining the information hierarchy, designing the funding assessment, limit setup and first-advance flows, then refining the experience through pre-launch testing and post-launch feedback.

WHY IT WAS HARD

The funding assessment, reusable limit and first advance were connected, but they were not the same decision. Verified revenue affected the accuracy of the available limit, while the percentage of daily sales selected for payments changed how much the merchant could access. Agents needed to understand these relationships well enough to explain them accurately during a live funding conversation.

The process

Mapping the Flex Advance funding journey

Flex Advance was a new product, so there was no established Partner Hub journey to reuse. I mapped merchant, agent and system actions before moving into detailed screen design. This clarified the order and dependencies between account creation, revenue verification, funding assessment, limit setup and the first advance, including which decisions belonged to the agent and which needed the merchant's agreement.

The map gave me the structure of the journey, but it also exposed the point that needed the most explanation. The funding assessment did not simply return an amount for the merchant to take. It established what they could access, while the percentage of daily sales selected for payments determined the reusable limit. Making that relationship understandable became the focus of the detailed design.

Making the product mechanics understandable

With the overall journey defined, the main screen-level challenge was showing the relationship between two values: the percentage of daily sales used for payments and the reusable account limit. A higher percentage made a larger limit available, but it also meant a greater share of the merchant's daily sales would go towards payments. The interface needed to make that trade-off clear rather than presenting the percentage and limit as unrelated figures.

I placed the percentage control and resulting account limit in the same card so the cause and effect could be seen together. The slider labels explained the range from lower payments to the highest available limit. As the agent moved the slider, the highlighted limit updated directly beneath it and repeated the selected percentage, making it easier to explain how one value affected the other before the merchant agreed to it.

Establishing the limit did not automatically mean taking funding. I kept the first advance as a separate step so agents could establish the reusable limit first, then help the merchant choose an advance amount and understand its cost before confirming it.

Designing the funding assessment and wider journey

Once the key product mechanics were clear, I designed the remaining screens needed to turn the map into a complete journey. Stages such as business details, ownership details and review could build on established Partner Hub patterns. Reusing familiar structures kept those interactions consistent and allowed me to focus more design effort on the parts that were specific to Flex Advance.

The funding assessment was the main branching point. Open Banking used verified revenue and produced the most accurate limit, while Estimated Figures provided an alternative when Open Banking was not possible. I designed the screen so agents could understand the purpose of each route, choose the appropriate one and continue into the relevant data-collection flow.

I connected the account-creation, assessment, limit-setup and first-advance screens in one clickable prototype. This allowed agents to experience the sequence as a complete workflow rather than reviewing individual screens in isolation.

Testing the prototype with partner agents

Before launch, I ran moderated sessions with partner sales agents using a clickable prototype. I created the discussion guide and scenarios, facilitated each session and synthesised the findings. The sessions combined scenario-based walkthroughs, think-aloud observation and questions about how agents prepared for and supported merchant conversations.

Live conversations placed agents under pressure. Agents told me it was easy to forget product details while speaking with a merchant, particularly when explaining payments, fees and how the reusable limit worked.

Revenue inputs needed to match real conversations. The original journey asked for annual revenue only, but agents often discussed revenue in monthly terms.

The assessment routes served different levels of certainty. Open Banking used verified revenue to produce the most accurate limit. Agents still needed an alternative when it was not possible, as well as a non-committal way to discuss indicative funding before a merchant was ready to apply.

Translating the findings into the launch design

For launch, I visually prioritised Open Banking within the funding assessment as it used verified revenue to produce the most accurate limit. It was pre-selected, highlighted with colour and labelled “Fastest route,” with a checklist explaining its benefits. Estimated Figures remained available when Open Banking was not possible.

I also allowed agents to enter revenue monthly or annually, matching the language they used with merchants while keeping the assessment consistent.

Supporting live merchant conversations

To reduce the pressure on agents to remember every product detail, I added contextual FAQs covering common questions about payments, fees and the reusable limit. These acted as prompts for the agent and supported clearer, more consistent explanations to merchants.

One FAQ on the limit setup screen included an interactive example calculator. Agents could adjust a hypothetical first-advance amount and show its indicative fixed fee, percentage of daily sales and total payback. It supported the conversation without starting or confirming an advance.

Separating indicative conversations from applications

Design iteration · Not yet shipped

The Product Manager reported back from the data she could see that 80% of agents were selecting Estimated Figures rather than Open Banking. This was a PM-reported post-launch finding; it did not establish why agents chose that route. Technical issues with Open Banking may have contributed, but the launch design also kept both options in adjacent cards of the same size. Despite the pre-selection, colour treatment and “Fastest route” label, the two routes still had similar structural weight.

I revised the hierarchy so Open Banking became the sole primary card. Estimated Figures moved into a secondary application route, and the non-committal Funding Preview became a separate tool for discussing indicative amounts without creating or updating an application.

Three routes became clearer. Open Banking became the primary application route because it used verified revenue data to establish an accurate Flex Advance limit. Funding preview became a separate, non-committal tool so agents could enter estimated figures and discuss a possible amount without saving it to the merchant's account or starting an application. Can't do Open Banking remained an application route, taking the agent to a dedicated estimated-figures screen where self-declared monthly or annual revenue could be used to create a quote linked to the application.

Processor selection also moved into its own step, showing only processors associated with the merchant rather than asking agents to search through irrelevant options.

From a new product to a working journey

The work took Flex Advance from a new funding product with no Partner Hub journey to a live experience actively used by partner agents. They can create an account, establish a reusable limit and take a first advance on the merchant’s behalf.

I also influenced what the launch experience supported. Advocating for the first-advance calculator brought cost exploration into scope, so agents could discuss an indicative amount and its payback before committing to an advance.

Delivery created a basis for further product decisions. The Product Manager reported from the data available to her that 80% of agents selected Estimated Figures. I used that finding to revisit the assessment structure and distinguish a non-committal funding conversation from an application.

The subsequent assessment redesign and separate Funding Preview are designed, not yet shipped; their effect on behaviour remains to be tested.

From design to shipped product

DELIVERY

I worked closely with engineers to turn the Flex Advance journey into production behaviour, accounting for Open Banking states, processor data, funding rules and the relationship between daily-sales percentage and reusable limit. I stayed involved during implementation to resolve edge cases and ensure the calculations, choices and explanations remained accurate in the shipped experience.

What I learned

Post-launch behaviour challenged an assumption in the launch design. Although Open Banking was pre-selected, highlighted and labelled “Fastest route,” the Product Manager reported from the data available to her that 80% of agents were choosing Estimated Figures. Technical problems with Open Banking may have contributed, but the behaviour prompted me to question whether visual styling was enough to establish a hierarchy when the layout presented two routes as equivalent. The revised design still needs testing to distinguish the effect of hierarchy from technical reliability and agent preferences.

I also learned that an indicative conversation, an evidence-based assessment and a confirmed first advance are different tasks, with different levels of certainty and commitment. Organising the experience around what the agent was trying to achieve, rather than only the type of data being entered, gave each one a clearer and more honest role. Separating these levels of certainty is a core design decision, not a detail.
